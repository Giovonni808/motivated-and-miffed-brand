## 1. Short-form script: TikTok and Reels

**Format:** credibility anchor then payload. Text overlay on b-roll, or talking head if available.
**Effort:** under an hour with existing editing-timeline b-roll.
**Length:** ~40 seconds.

**HOOK (0-3s, on screen and spoken):**
> "I had a couple hours to cut a wedding video before the reception started. Same night. Every time."

**Body (3-10s):**
> That job taught me something no productivity book did. You cannot find focus. You can only remove
> options.

**Payload (10-28s):**
> Three things I did then and still do now.
>
> One. I decided the ending before I shot a frame. If I know where it lands, I stop collecting footage
> I will never use.
>
> Two. I built the structure empty and filled it in. Five markers on a blank timeline. I never start
> from a blank page.
>
> Three. Anything that did not serve the ending got cut. Not saved for later. Cut.

**Turn (28-36s):**
> None of that was inspiring. It was a deadline I could not move. Ten years later it is still the only
> method I trust.

**CTA (36-42s):**
> I wrote up how I run this on a normal week, when there is no wedding forcing it. It is this week's
> Motivated and Miffed, the issue called "You're Not in Flow. You're Just Grinding." Link in bio.

**Production notes:** b-roll of a timeline being cut down works better than a face here, since the
hook is about the constraint. Dynamic captions. Keep the three rules on screen as they are spoken.

## 2. LinkedIn post

**Format:** workflow authority. Plain text, no hashtag block.

> I saved a museum $30,000 in one year by doing something unglamorous. I wrote down how we made videos.
>
> At Shangri La I produced video for exhibitions and education programs. The work was good. The process
> was invisible. Every project effectively started over because nobody had written down how the last one
> worked.
>
> So I mapped it. Where footage lived, who approved what, which steps we repeated every time. Then I cut
> the steps that existed only because someone had once done them that way.
>
> The $30K was not a negotiation or a cheaper vendor. It was the same team no longer rebuilding the same
> process from memory.
>
> I think about that a lot now, doing communications for a city council office where the deadlines are
> shorter and the work is public. The pattern holds. Most creative teams are not slow because the people
> are slow. They are slow because the process only exists in somebody's head.
>
> If your team cannot describe how work actually moves, that is not a documentation problem. That is the
> cost, and you are already paying it.
>
> I write about this weekly in Motivated and Miffed, for creatives with actual deadlines. This week is on
> why grinding keeps getting mistaken for focus.
